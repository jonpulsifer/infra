At work, I help make sure the computers running things are really the computers we think they are, running the software we expect. I also build a smaller, much noisier version of this at home. Here's the story without the server-room vocabulary.

01 / The problem

“It says it’s Santa.”

A label on a package is a claim, not proof of what’s inside.

📦

To: The North Pole

From: Definitely Santa

Anyone can print a label. Computers can print very convincing labels.

Not verified
Presenter notes · 0:50

A computer announces its name when it connects to a network. But names, serial numbers and even a friendly login screen can be copied. At a big company, assuming that every machine is who it says it is would be like leaving the workshop unlocked because someone wore a red hat.

02 / Three questions

Trust is a checklist,
not a feeling.

One · identity

Who are you?

Is this the machine we invited?

Two · integrity

How did you wake up?

Did you start with approved software?

Three · permission

What may you do?

Only the job you came here to do.

A yes to one question is not a yes to all three.

Presenter notes · 1:00

This is the whole talk. A password might tell us who signed in but not whether their computer has been tampered with. A correctly booted laptop still shouldn't be allowed to open every door. Security is about answering all three questions independently, then answering them again when things change.

03 / Who are you?

A key that won’t leave home.

A computer can keep a secret in a special chip called a TPM — a tiny, stubborn vault.

🔐

The secret stays put.

Ask the chip to sign a fresh challenge. Check its answer against an identity we already trust.

Like a unique wax seal, but you can't copy the stamp by reading the letter.

Presenter notes · 1:00

TPM stands for Trusted Platform Module. In my lab, a machine's hardware keys are checked against an approved identity or its manufacturer's proof. It must actually use that key to answer a new challenge; just telling us the key's name isn't enough. And there's a very human first step: deciding which machine we meant to invite.

Lab story: fleetd attestation

04 / How did you wake up?

Ask the boot for its receipts.

The chip records clues about the startup. The computer signs a fresh snapshot; a verifier checks it against an allowed recipe.

🧾

Tonight’s menu

  • Signed starting instructions
  • Expected boot ingredients
  • No surprise substitutions

Secure Boot checks signatures; attestation reports what booted. Neither promises a bug-free computer.

Presenter notes · 1:05

Think of Secure Boot as a bouncer checking who signed the program before letting it start. Attestation is different: the TPM gives a signed report about the startup, and our verifier compares that report with policy. We check the evidence; we do not blindly take the machine's word for it. This is useful evidence of boot state, not a magical guarantee about everything the computer does afterward.

Lab story: node lifecycle and boot policy

05 / What may you do?

A name badge, not a master key.

Once a machine passes its checks, it can get a short-lived identity. The little programs on it get their own.

🏷️

Workshop rules

Wrapping crew → wrapping paper.
Cookie crew → cookie tin.
Neither → Santa’s entire address book.

No forever-password taped to the sleigh.

Presenter notes · 1:00

In the lab, SPIRE issues identities to approved machines and the workloads running on them. A service can use its identity to get access without carrying a long-lived cloud key file. This isn't blanket permission: access policies still decide which service may reach which resource. Identity is how we ask for the right permission, not a substitute for permission.

Lab story: node and workload identity

06 / The real trick

Trust has an expiry date.

A good answer yesterday is not a free pass today. Ask again.

⏳

If the recipe changes…

A machine that fails a fresh check can lose its place at the table. Its credentials stop renewing; its work is kept apart.

Not instant erasure: already-issued credentials can remain valid until they expire.

Presenter notes · 1:00

My lab re-checks running machines, not just new ones. An authenticated bad boot on a worker leads to quarantine and blocks credential renewal. The system also removes registration, but an already-issued credential may live until expiry. We have to think about that delay honestly; 'revoke' is a process, not a magic wand. Some critical machines have a different failure response so we don't accidentally turn off the whole workshop.

Lab story: re-checking a running node

07 / The job

So what do I do at work?

I help build the rules and plumbing that let infrastructure earn access — and lose it when it should.

🛠️

Infrastructure Security Engineer

Make the safe path practical for the teams building things. Find the places where a computer is trusted just because it showed up.

The lab is my personal workshop; it isn't a diagram of ElevenLabs systems.

Presenter notes · 0:55

This deck uses the home lab as the example, not a description of internal ElevenLabs systems. The family-sized version of my role is: help people build and run useful things, while making sure identity, access and evidence are actually doing their jobs. The goal isn't to make computers scary. It's to make relying on them a little less like guessing.

The takeaway · ~8 minutes

Trust, but ask for receipts.

Who are you? How did you wake up? What may you do? When do we ask again?
